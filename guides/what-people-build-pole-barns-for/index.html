What people build pole barns for — and what each use needs

Updated 18 Aug 2026 · 5 min read ·By the Pole Barn Cost editorial team
Short answer

The use decides three things that cannot be changed later — sidewall height, door width and clear span — and almost nothing about the price per square foot. A pole barn man cave and a pole barn for equipment cost the same to build; they differ in what gets finished inside.

Every use of a pole barn shares the same frame and diverges on three dimensions. Those three are decided at the quote stage and cannot be revisited afterwards, which is why this page is about dimensions rather than about decor.

The three things the use decides

Sidewall height. A motorhome needs sixteen feet at the eave; a workshop with a lift needs fourteen; storage for a mower is comfortable at ten. Height is set when the posts go in the ground and cannot be raised later.

Door width and placement. Openings sit inside a bay, between posts. A door for a tractor with a raised loader is a different specification from a door for a car, and both have to be planned before the frame is designed.

Clear span. The trusses carry the roof wall to wall, so nothing stands in the middle — which is what makes a court, an arena or an open shop possible at all.

Storage and equipment

The default use and the cheapest one. A bare shell on a gravel floor keeps machinery dry and asks nothing else. What people regret here is depth: twenty feet parks a vehicle and leaves a corridor, thirty leaves a working area behind it.

Workshop and hobby space

Add a slab, insulation and wiring, and the building stops being a shed. The order matters more than the budget — a vapour barrier under the roof panels costs almost nothing during the build and cannot be added afterwards without taking the roof off.

RV and tall vehicles

An RV pole barn is a height problem wearing a floor-area disguise. Fourteen-foot door, sixteen-foot sidewall, and enough length to walk around the vehicle rather than squeeze past it. Get those right and forty feet of length is usually plenty.

Man cave, gym, court

These are the uses where the honest answer disappoints slightly: the shell is cheap, the interior is not. Finishing a post-frame building to a comfortable, heated, lined space costs what finishing any building costs — the saving was in the structure, and it stays there.

What post-frame does give you is volume. A half basketball court needs about 50 by 42 feet of clear floor and a ceiling well above the backboard; a full high-school court is 84 by 50 plus run-off. Getting that span without interior posts is straightforward here and awkward in almost any other method at the same price.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

What size is a pole barn basketball court?

A half court needs roughly 50 by 42 feet of clear floor; a full high-school court is 84 by 50 plus run-off and a ceiling above backboard height. Post-frame does that span without interior posts, which is the reason it comes up at all.

What is a pole barn used for?

Equipment and vehicle storage first, workshops second, then everything from an RV shelter to a home gym or a hobby space. The frame is the same in all of them — the use changes the height, the door and the interior, not the structure.

What does an rv pole barn need?

Height, mostly. A 14-foot door and a 16-foot sidewall are the usual starting point, and both are decided when the posts go in. Length matters less than people expect — 40 feet covers most motorhomes with room to walk round.

Can a pole barn be a man cave?

It is one of the common uses, and the honest version of the answer is that it costs what any finished interior costs. The shell is cheap; insulation, lining, heat and wiring are the same price they would be in a house.

What size pole barn for a home gym?

Far less than people think — 20x30 takes equipment and floor space. Height is the constraint for anything overhead, and a slab is not optional.
