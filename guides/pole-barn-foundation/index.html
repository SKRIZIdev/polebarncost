Pole barn foundation — options, depth and what code requires

Updated 18 Aug 2026 · 6 min read ·By the Pole Barn Cost editorial team
Short answer

A pole barn has no continuous foundation — the posts carry the building. They go into the ground, onto a concrete footing in the hole, or onto a bracket fixed to concrete. And contrary to what most of the industry writes, the code does not require going below the frost line — it lists four acceptable methods of frost protection and exempts free-standing accessory buildings of 600 square feet or less with an eave height of 10 feet or less entirely.

A pole barn has no foundation in the sense most people mean. There is no continuous footing under the walls, because the walls carry nothing — the posts do. What there is, under each post, is one of three arrangements.

The three ways to set a post

Post in the ground. The traditional method and the cheapest. A treated post goes into a hole, usually onto a concrete pad, with a concrete collar around it to resist uplift. One regional contractor publishes $75 to $150 of concrete per post for the pad and collar — their figure, for their market, not an industry average.

Post on a precast footing. The same hole, with an engineered concrete pad at the bottom. The load is spread over a wider area, which matters on soft ground.

Bracket on a pier or slab. The post never touches soil: a steel bracket anchors it to a concrete pier or to the slab itself. This removes the failure mode people worry about — timber in wet ground — and costs the most. The same contractor prices the upgrade at $200 to $400 and up per column, roughly triple the in-ground arrangement.

What the code actually says about frost

This is where most of what is written about pole barns is wrong, including on builders' own websites.

The requirement is not «go below the frost line». IRC 2024, section R403.1.4.1, reads:

Except where otherwise protected from frost, foundation walls, piers and other permanent supports of buildings and structures shall be protected from frost by one or more of the following methods: Extended below the frost line specified in Table R301.2. Constructed in accordance with Section R403.3. Constructed in accordance with ASCE 32. Erected on solid rock.

Four methods, any one of which satisfies the requirement. Going below the frost line is the first and the most common, and in cold regions with deep frost it is often the cheapest — but it is a choice from a list, not a universal rule.

There is also a separate minimum that applies regardless: exterior footings are placed not less than 12 inches below undisturbed ground.

The exception that covers a lot of small barns

In the same section:

Protection of free-standing accessory structures with an area of 600 square feet (56 m2) or less, of light-frame construction, with an eave height of 10 feet (3048 mm) or less shall not be required.

Three conditions, and they work only together — the building has to be free-standing, accessory (not a dwelling), and under both limits. Post-frame is light-frame construction, so a detached 24x24 storage building with a 10-foot eave falls inside this exemption. For structures that are not light-frame, the threshold is 400 square feet.

Two cautions before you use this. Local amendments override the model code, and plenty of counties have them. And an exemption from frost protection is not an argument for skipping it where frost heave is real — it is permission, not advice.

How deep, in practice

Where frost depth does govern, the practice figures run deeper than the frost line itself. The contractor table above lists, for its region: frost at 36 to 48 inches with posts set 48 to 60; mountain Colorado at 48-plus with posts at 54 to 72; Montana at 36 to 48-plus with posts at 54 to 72.

Those are one company's numbers for its own market, and they are useful as a sense of scale rather than as a specification. Your number comes from the engineering for your building and from the county table.

The floor is a separate decision

A slab is not part of the foundation here. The building stands without one, which is why gravel floors are common and why the slab so often falls out of an estimate.

Worth knowing before you postpone it: pouring later costs meaningfully more than pouring during the build. One contractor puts the difference at 20 to 30 per cent, because access for concrete trucks is restricted once the building is up — often requiring a pump — and finishing against existing posts and walls is slower work.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

Do pole barn footings have to go below the frost line?

Not necessarily. IRC R403.1.4.1 says supports «shall be protected from frost by one or more of the following methods» and then lists four — below the frost line, a frost-protected shallow foundation per R403.3, ASCE 32, or bearing on solid rock. Going deep is the common answer, not the only legal one. Your county decides which it will accept.

What are the pole barn foundation options?

Post set directly in the ground; post set on a precast concrete footing at the bottom of the hole; and a bracket on a concrete pier or slab that keeps the timber entirely out of the ground. They differ in cost and in how the timber is protected, not in how the building stands up.

Is there a size below which frost protection is not required?

Yes. The code exempts free-standing accessory structures of light-frame construction with an area of 600 square feet or less and an eave height of 10 feet or less. All three conditions have to be true together — detached, accessory, and under both limits. For structures that are not light-frame the threshold is 400 square feet.

How deep do pole barn posts go?

Where frost depth governs, contractors in cold regions report setting posts deeper than the frost line itself — for example 48 to 60 inches where frost is 36 to 48. That is a contractor's practice figure, not a code number, and the code separately requires exterior footings to sit at least 12 inches below undisturbed ground.

Do pole barn footings need concrete?

Not always, but usually. A concrete pad at the base of the hole spreads the load, and a collar around the post resists uplift. The alternative — a bracket on a concrete pier — is more expensive and keeps the post out of the soil entirely.
