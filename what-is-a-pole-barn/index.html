What is a pole barn? Post-frame construction explained

Updated 17 Aug 2026 · 7 min read ·By the Pole Barn Cost editorial team
Short answer

A pole barn is a building whose weight is carried by posts set into the ground or on brackets, instead of by a continuous foundation wall. The posts stand 8 to 12 feet apart, so there is far less framing and no footing under every wall — which is why it costs $20 to $60 a square foot against $100 and up for conventional construction. The industry calls it post-frame; everyone else calls it a pole barn.

Almost every building you have lived in works the same way: a continuous foundation wall carries the load, and studs every 16 inches carry the wall. A pole barn does neither.

Its weight sits on posts standing 8 to 12 feet apart. That is the entire idea, and everything else — the low cost, the fast build, the open floor — follows from it.

How post-frame construction works

Large solid-sawn posts or laminated columns are set into the ground below the frost line, or bolted to concrete piers with brackets that keep the timber out of the soil. Trusses land on top of the posts. Horizontal girts tie the posts together and give the cladding something to fix to. Steel panels close the envelope.

There is no footing under every wall because there is no wall carrying anything. The load goes down the posts, and only the posts need support.

The National Frame Building Association — the body that publishes the accepted practices for this method — defines it as an engineered wood-frame system using posts or laminated columns in place of studs, steel framing or masonry. That word engineered matters at the permit office, and we come back to it below.

Why it costs less

Three reasons, in order of how much they save.

Less foundation. A continuous frost-depth footing around a 40x60 building is a substantial concrete pour. Posts on piers are not.

Less framing. Posts at 8 to 12 feet instead of studs at 16 inches means a fraction of the members, and fewer, larger pieces go up faster than many small ones.

Less labour. An experienced crew erects a basic frame in two or three days. Labour on a conventional building of the same footprint is measured in weeks.

The result is $20 to $60 per square foot against roughly $100 and up for conventional construction. The gap narrows as you finish the inside, and disappears entirely if you turn it into a house.

What you get that stick framing does not give you

A floor with nothing in the middle of it. Engineered trusses clear-span well over 100 feet. For anything you are likely to build, there is no reason to put a post in the middle of the space.

Deep wall cavities. Posts 8 to 12 feet apart leave the wall thickness free for insulation, so a post-frame building insulates well — when someone actually insulates it. Left bare, the same building sweats, and that is the most common complaint about pole barns generally.

A shell you can extend. Adding a lean-to runs $15 to $17 per square foot, because you are attaching to posts rather than cutting into a structural wall.

Where it is the wrong answer

If you need a basement. Post-frame does not do a full basement in any sensible way.

If code or a covenant says otherwise. Some jurisdictions and most subdivisions have opinions about post-frame residential construction. Check before, not after.

If you are building a house and have only priced the shell. Living quarters run $100 to $250 per square foot in post-frame. The shell being cheap is real; it just does not carry over to the finished house.

Pole barn, post-frame, barndominium

Three words for overlapping things, and they cause a lot of confusion in quotes.

Pole barn is the everyday term for the building method — and originally for the buildings themselves, put up with actual poles.

Post-frame is the same method in industry language, and what you will see on engineering drawings and kit specifications. A listing that says post frame pole barn is not describing two things — it is one building with both names on it, usually because the seller wants to be found under either.

Barndominium is a post-frame building finished as a dwelling. It is not a grade of pole barn; it is a house that happens to use this frame, priced accordingly.

Why is it called a pole barn

Because the first ones were built with poles. In the 1930s American Midwest, telephone and utility poles were cheap and plentiful, and farmers set them straight into the ground and roofed between them — no foundation, no stud wall, a building for the price of the roof. The poles are gone from modern kits, replaced by square laminated columns, but the name stayed with the method.

Which also answers what is pole barn framing, in one line: it is that same idea, with the poles squared off and the engineering written down.

Pole barn against the alternatives

Four comparisons come up constantly, and each turns on one thing.

Metal building vs pole barn. A steel building carries the roof on a bolted steel frame and usually needs a continuous slab designed as part of the structure. Post-frame carries it on posts in the ground. Steel wins on very wide clear spans; post-frame wins on price at ordinary sizes.

Stick built vs pole barn. Stick framing puts studs every 16 inches on a continuous footing under every wall. That footing is the cost difference, and it is most of it.

Shed vs pole barn. A shed is a finished product delivered whole, and below roughly 200 square feet it is the cheaper answer — the fixed costs of prep, delivery and permit do not shrink with the building.

Barn style pole building. A styling choice, not a construction one: gambrel roof lines and board-and-batten cladding on the same post frame.

FAQ

Questions & Answers

Answered from the same sources as the tables above — each figure is linked on the page it comes from.

Whats a pole barn, in plain terms?

A shed or barn whose weight sits on posts rather than on walls. That single difference removes the continuous foundation, most of the framing, and a large part of the cost.

What is a pole barn, in one sentence?

A building carried by widely spaced posts instead of a continuous foundation and stud walls — which is what makes it cheaper to build and quicker to put up.

What is an enclosed pole barn?

One with walls on all four sides, as opposed to an open shelter or a carport. The frame is identical; the difference is the envelope, and it is most of the cost difference.

What is an rv pole barn?

A pole barn sized for a motorhome, which means the height matters more than the floor: a 14-foot door and a 16-foot sidewall are the usual starting point, and neither can be changed after the posts are set.

Does a pole barn need a foundation?

Not a continuous one. Posts either go into the ground below the frost line or sit on concrete piers with brackets that keep the timber out of the soil. A concrete floor is a separate decision — useful, and priced separately at $4 to $6 a square foot.
